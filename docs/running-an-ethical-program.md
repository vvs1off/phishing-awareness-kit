# Running an ethical, consent-based awareness program

This kit contains **no phishing-simulation tool**. If your organization chooses to run simulated exercises, use an established, reputable platform, and follow these principles.

## Principles
1. **Transparency.** Tell staff the program exists, what it involves, and why. Surprise is a design choice, not a requirement.
2. **Purpose is learning.** The goal is better judgment and more reporting, not catching people out.
3. **No shaming.** Never publish names, rank people, or use results in performance reviews.
4. **Reporting rate over click rate.** Celebrate people who report; treat clicks as a signal to improve training and controls.
5. **Respect.** Avoid lures that exploit fear or hardship (bonus cuts, layoffs, health scares, family emergencies).
6. **Teachable moments.** Anyone who clicks sees immediate, friendly guidance, not a punishment screen.

## Legal and HR considerations
> Not legal advice. Consult counsel in each jurisdiction.

- **Works councils / employee representatives:** consult them where required.
- **Data protection:** decide what is collected, why, how long it is kept, and who can access it. Minimize individual-level data; prefer aggregated reporting. Consider a data protection impact assessment.
- **Employment terms:** align with your policy and contracts; do not use results for discipline.
- **Accessibility and fairness:** make training available in plain language and accessible formats.
- **Third-party platforms:** review contracts, data location, and sub-processors.

## How NOT to shame employees
- ❌ Leaderboards of "worst clickers"
- ❌ Mandatory punitive retraining after a single click
- ❌ Public emails naming individuals
- ✅ Private, kind, short feedback
- ✅ Thanking reporters publicly (with consent)
- ✅ Fixing technical gaps (filtering, MFA) so people are not your only defense

## Measuring success
- Reporting rate and time to report
- Reduction in credential submissions over time
- Staff confidence (anonymous survey)
- Time to contain real incidents
